Community Artificial Reef Programs: A Practical Overview

Prepared for coastal community volunteers, updated March 2026.

Why artificial reefs exist

Artificial reefs are deliberately placed structures that give marine life a hard surface to colonize in areas where the seabed is soft sediment. When deployed correctly, they concentrate fish, support algae and invertebrate growth, and can relieve pressure on natural reef systems by drawing recreational diving and fishing away from fragile sites.

Common materials and their trade-offs

Decommissioned vessels, concrete modules, and purpose-built steel structures are the three dominant approaches. Concrete is cheap and stable but takes years to develop full coverage. Vessels must be cleaned of oils, fuels, and floatables before sinking, and access ports are cut for diver safety. Steel structures colonize fastest but need thickness checks over time.

Permitting basics

Most jurisdictions require an environmental assessment, a siting study covering currents and depth, and coordination with maritime authorities so the site appears on navigation charts. Community groups should budget for multi-year monitoring, not just deployment day.

Monitoring without a marine biology degree

Simple protocols work: fixed photo quadrants, seasonal fish counts along a transect line, and visibility logs. Consistency matters more than sophistication. Ten minutes of observations every month outperforms a single exhaustive annual survey.
